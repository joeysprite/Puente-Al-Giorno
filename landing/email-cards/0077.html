Español

Te quiero

“I want you”

I love you — the warm everyday form, used with partners, family and close friends alike.

Te quiero mucho, hermanita.

I love you so much, sis.

Italiano

Ti voglio bene

“I want good for you”

I love you — affection for family and friends, and explicitly NOT the romantic declaration.

Ti voglio bene, sorellina.

I love you, little sis.

El puente · Il ponte

Both languages soften love with a verb of wanting, and then aim it differently: Spanish wants the PERSON — te quiero, I want you — while Italian wants GOOD FOR the person, ti voglio bene. The forms look interchangeable and are not, because the two scales run opposite. In Spanish, te quiero is the everyday warmth couples live in and te amo is the heavy one, kept for weight. In Italian, ti amo is what partners say, and ti voglio bene marks affection that stops short of romance. The same softening move is tender in one language and a demotion in the other.

La trampa · La trappola

ES → IT

The costly one: saying ti voglio bene to a romantic partner can read as a let-down, affection without romance. If you mean it romantically, the word is ti amo.

IT → ES

Reaching straight for te amo is understood, but the weight varies by region: in Spain te amo is more common, while in Latin America it is heavier and often saved for major emotional moments. Te quiero carries most everyday romantic weight.
