Había una vez

“there was a time”

Once upon a time — the fixed opener of fairy tales and stories.

Había una vez una princesa que vivía en un castillo.

Once upon a time there was a princess who lived in a castle.

Italiano

C'era una volta

“there was a time/turn”

Once upon a time — the fixed opener of fairy tales and stories.

C'era una volta un re che aveva tre figlie.

Once upon a time there was a king who had three daughters.

El puente · Il ponte

Identical openers hiding the deepest grammar split between the two languages: the existential. Spanish builds 'there was' on haber (había); Italian builds it on esserci (c'era) — and although avere is haber's direct cognate, Italian never uses it existentially. Bonus false friend: volta is the cognate of Spanish vuelta ('turn'), not of vez — Italian literally counts occasions in turns, the same volta that was just flipped in entry 0028's frittata.

La trampa · La trappola

ES → IT

The haber→avere cognate pull produces 'aveva una volta' or 'ha molte cose' for existentials. Italian existentials take esserci: c'è, c'era. If you can say c'era una volta, you already own the pattern — lean on it.

IT → ES

Italians reach for essere and produce 'era una vez' — half-camouflaged by the archaic 'Érase una vez', so it slips by, but productive Spanish existentials take haber: hay, había.
