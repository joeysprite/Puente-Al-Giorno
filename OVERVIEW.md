# Un puente al giorno — Executive Overview

*The five-minute picture. For the technical detail, see `OPERATIONS.md` (services
and runbook), `HOW-IT-WORKS.md` (internal architecture), `SCHEMA.md` (entry
format), and `SCHEDULING.md` (how a date becomes an entry).*

---

## The product

One Spanish expression and one Italian expression per day, paired — plus **the
bridge**: what carries across between them, what doesn't, and the exact mistake
a speaker of one makes in the other.

The bridge is the moat. A Spanish-only or Italian-only account structurally
cannot produce it, because it only exists in the space between two languages.
Anyone can list idioms; almost nobody can tell you that *poner toda la carne en
el asador* (admiring: going all in) and *mettere troppa carne al fuoco*
(scolding: overreaching) are the same words with opposite verdicts.

English is a support rail, not the frame. The audience is learners of either
language, and the warnings are **directional** — what trips a Spanish speaker in
Italian is not what trips an Italian in Spanish.

**Who it's for:** intermediate learners. 84% of approved entries are CEFR B1–B2,
which targets the intermediate plateau — the point where beginner resources are
too easy, native material is still too hard, and what's missing is precisely
idiom, register, and the things that make a speaker sound like a person rather
than a textbook. This is a description of the bank as built, not an aspiration.

**The long game:** the daily feed tests hundreds of pairings on real readers. The
ones that land become a bilingual tear-off calendar. The feed is how the calendar
gets written.

---

## Three surfaces, in deliberate order

**1. Instagram — first, because it is cheapest to be wrong on.**
A daily post costs nothing to skip and teaches you what resonates. No promise is
made to anyone. This is where the product finds its voice before committing.

**2. The website — second, as the back catalogue.**
Posts accumulate into a browsable archive. Someone who finds the Instagram
account comes here and reads everything so far. The site is *backfilled* from
what has already been posted, so it is useful from day one without requiring a
separate content stream.

**3. The email — last, because it is the only real promise.**
A daily email says "we will be in your inbox tomorrow." That is a commitment you
cannot quietly walk back, so it comes only when the content pipeline is proven.

---

## The three services, and what each actually does

| Service | Role | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Netlify** | Serves the website | The public front door. Hosts the archive, the landing page, and the signup form. Does **not** build anything — it only serves files already generated and committed. |
| **Buttondown** | Holds the mailing list and sends the email | Where every signup lands. Stores each subscriber's starting language, which is what makes the directional warnings possible. The list is the durable asset — followers rent, subscribers own. |
| **Instagram** | The daily audience surface | Where the product is discovered and where the format gets proven. `@unpuentealgiorno`. Posting is manual by design; there is no auto-poster. |

Behind all three sits **GitHub**, which is the actual engine: it holds the
content, runs the checks, and rebuilds the site on a schedule. The three services
above are, deliberately, dumb pipes. There are no servers anywhere in this
system.

**The critical dependency:** all three surfaces read from the same content bank,
and nothing may reach a reader until a **native speaker of that language has
signed off on it**. That single rule is enforced mechanically, not by memory —
and it is currently the only thing standing between the project and launch.

## Open questions worth exploring

*Raised 2026-07-30, not yet decided. Recorded here so they survive.*

**English may be training wheels, not a rail.** The current design glosses
everything through English. But a Spanish speaker learning Italian has no use for
an English gloss. Localizing `bridge.note` and the interference fields would shift
the product from *idioms explained to Anglophones* to *the bridge between two
Romance languages, for someone who already stands on one of them*. No entries
carry localized fields today; the schema would need extending.

**Reciprocal (tandem) learners as the core use case.** Entries are already
directional (`es_to_it`, `it_to_es`), so a single entry is a complete shared
lesson for a Spanish speaker learning Italian paired with an Italian learning
Spanish — each is the other's authority. See whitepaper §7 item 8: this may also
be a partial answer to the review-capacity constraint.

**Other language pairs (FR–PT, etc.).** The architecture generalizes trivially;
bridge *quality* does not. The pair must be close enough to generate false friends
and far enough that the traps matter. ES–IT is near-ideal; ES–PT may be too close.
Each new pair at minimum doubles reviewer cost.

**Who is competent to verify a bridge?** Currently nobody in `reviewers.json` —
every reviewer is registered with one language, and a bridge is a claim about two.
This is the sharpest known flaw in the trust model. Whitepaper §7 item 7.

## The risks worth naming

- **Single-person dependency.** One person writes, edits, designs, and ships. The
  documentation set exists partly to make that recoverable.
- **Reviewer capacity — now a recurring cost, not a one-off.** The initial 149
  have been reviewed, but a daily product generates review demand forever. The
  Italian reviewer's commercial terms are agreed; the Spanish reviewer's terms,
  and both credit lines, remain unsettled. Settle them before the next batch.
- **Unpushed work.** The repository is the durable copy; anything not pushed to
  GitHub exists in one place only. (This has already nearly bitten once.)
- **A dated public promise** (e.g. a launch date on a teaser) converts a soft
  timeline into a hard one. Less risky now that content is approved, but the
  email renderer is still unbuilt — do not date a promise the pipeline cannot
  keep.
- **Review depth is unrecorded.** The system records *that* a native approved an
  entry, never what they changed. There is no way to distinguish a careful
  reading from a fast sign-off, and no data on what review actually caught.
